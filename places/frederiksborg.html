Frederiksborg Castle

Frederiksborg Slot

The largest Renaissance palace in Scandinavia, built across three islets in a lake, and now the national portrait gallery.

Christian IV — the same king who built Rosenborg and dug Nyhavn — rebuilt his father’s hunting seat at Hillerød from 1600 into the grandest Renaissance palace in northern Europe, spread across three islets in the castle lake and approached over a sequence of bridges and gatehouses. It was a statement of Danish power at the moment Denmark still controlled both sides of the Øresund.

Most of it burned in December 1859. It was rebuilt largely because J.C. Jacobsen, the founder of Carlsberg, paid for it and then endowed it as a museum of national history — the same brewing money that stands behind the Glyptotek, the Gefion Fountain and the Little Mermaid. Denmark’s national portrait collection has hung here ever since.

What to see inside

Three things carry the visit. The chapel survived the fire intact: it was the coronation church of the absolutist kings, and its organ dates from 1610 and still works. The Great Hall on the top floor, with its coffered ceiling and tapestries. And the portrait galleries, which run chronologically from the 16th century to the present and are the closest thing Denmark has to a visual history of itself.

Getting there

S-train line A to Hillerød, every ten minutes, then bus 301 or 302 two stops — all covered by the Copenhagen Card. There is a pretty 17-minute walk around the lake as an alternative, which is the wrong choice in rain and wind.
