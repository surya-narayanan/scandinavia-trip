Fløyen, Fløibanen and Fjellveien

A funicular from the city centre to 320 metres, and a flat pedestrian road along the mountainside that almost no visitor finds.

Bergen is ringed by seven mountains and Fløyen is the accessible one. The Fløibanen funicular opened in 1918 and climbs from a station 150 metres behind Bryggen to 320 metres in about six minutes, on a track steep enough that the carriages are built as staircases. At the top there is a viewing plateau, a café, a troll-themed forest path, a small lake, goats in summer and genuinely flat walking.

Why the evening

The view faces west-north-west over the city, the harbour and the islands, so late light comes toward you across the water and the whole centre goes gold. At midday the same view is flat and hazy. Since the funicular runs to midnight there is no reason to take the worse hour.

Fjellveien and Skansen — the local route down

This is the part visitors miss. From the top, paths lead down the hillside and join Fjellveien, a broad level gravel road that runs along the mountainside above the whole city, from Sandviken in the north almost to Haukeland hospital in the south. It is a walking road, not a trail: flat, wide, and full of Bergensers exercising, with the city laid out below the whole way.

The descent, in numbers

The route down is Fløysvingene — a serpentine road that switchbacks off the summit — and the single most useful thing to know is that it is a road, not a staircase. Gravel and asphalt the whole way, described by the local tourist board as suitable for all ages and fitness levels. There are no steps to count.

The two numbers people quote are not alternatives and they are not in conflict — they measure different axes. 3 km is the ground you cover; 320 m is how far you drop. The summit really is 320 m above the sea, and the road really is about 3 km long, because a road that fell 320 m in a straight line would be unwalkable. That is the whole reason it switchbacks. Averaged out it works to a gradient of roughly 11%, which is a firm, continuous downhill but never a scramble.

  • Distance: about 3 km from the top station to the city centre by the standard route.
  • Descent: 320 m — the full height of the mountain, since the bottom station is at sea level.
  • Average gradient: ~11%, i.e. 1 m down for every 9 m forward.
  • Time: about 40 minutes at a brisk pace, an hour taking it gently and stopping for the view.
  • Steps: essentially none. It is a vehicle-width road surface throughout.
  • Junction: Fløysvingene meets Fjellveien by the drinking fountain, roughly 200 m north of where Fjellveien crosses the funicular line.

So the walk has three distinct characters rather than one: the switchback road down, then Fjellveien itself, which is flat, then a short steep zig-zag down through the wooden houses into Øvregaten. The only real constraint is knees — 320 m of continuous downhill is joint work regardless of surface — so anyone who would rather not simply takes the funicular back.

You do not have to walk the flat bit

This is worth saying plainly, because the standard description makes Fjellveien sound compulsory and it is not. Fløysvingene crosses Fjellveien on its way down; following Fjellveien along the hillside is a detour you choose, taken for the promenade and for the Skansen viewpoint. If you want continuous descent and then streets, cross straight over at the drinking fountain and keep going down — that shortens the walk to roughly 2.5 km and keeps it downhill nearly the whole way. Fjellveien sits at about 100 m, so the switchbacks above it do around two thirds of the drop and the zig-zag below it does the last third.

Either way the descent ends in the old town, not on a ring road. You come out on Øvregaten, the back lane behind Bryggen, a few doors from Schøtstuene and a short walk from Mariakirken — so the last stretch of the walk down the mountain is already a walk through medieval Bergen, and you can carry straight on into the Bryggen passages, Torgallmenningen and the harbour without a break.

About halfway along it, above the Skansen fire station, is the best free viewpoint over the city centre. From there a zig-zag road drops steeply down through the oldest surviving wooden houses in Bergen — small painted timber cottages clinging to the slope on lanes too narrow for cars — and puts you out near the funicular base and Øvregaten. Allow about an hour from the summit at an easy pace. It is a long descent, so anyone who should not be doing 320 vertical metres downhill takes the funicular.
