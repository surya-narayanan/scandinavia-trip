Stockholm City Hall

Stadshuset

Eight million bricks on the waterfront, and inside it a hall lined with eighteen million gold mosaic tiles.

Ragnar Östberg won the competition in 1907 and spent sixteen years building what is probably the defining work of Swedish National Romanticism: a dark red brick mass on the water at Kungsholmen, with a square tower carrying three golden crowns, deliberately recalling both a Venetian palazzo and a Nordic castle. About eight million bricks, many hand-made and deliberately irregular.

The Blue Hall, which is not blue

Östberg intended to render the great courtyard hall in blue plaster, saw the raw brick during construction, and changed his mind — but the name had stuck and was never changed. This is where the Nobel banquet is held each 10 December for about 1,300 guests, and the grand staircase was reportedly designed after the architect had his wife walk up and down in an evening gown until the tread and rise let her descend without looking at her feet.

The Golden Hall

The room people come for. Around eighteen million gilded glass mosaic tiles cover the walls in an Art Deco Byzantine scheme by Einar Forseth, with the Queen of Lake Mälaren enthroned on the north wall between representations of East and West. It was controversial on completion — the figures are stylised and the composition crowded, and the Queen’s head is famously cut off by the ceiling where the design ran out of wall.

The tour also takes in the Council Chamber, with an open timber roof modelled on a Viking longhouse, and the Prince’s Gallery, whose fresco of the view over Stockholm was painted by Prince Eugen — a real painter as well as a royal.
