The Fram Museum

Frammuseet

The strongest wooden ship ever built, preserved whole inside a museum built around her — and you go aboard.

Day 6 · Thu Aug 13 · 10:00–11:30Ninety minutes, and the point is that you walk the decks and go below — this is not a ship behind glass. Kon-Tiki is next door if the group is enjoying itself.
The shipFram, launched 1892, designed by Colin Archer
VoyagesNansen’s Arctic drift 1893–96; Amundsen to the South Pole 1910–12
Cost~NOK 180
Next doorThe Kon-Tiki Museum, +45 min, ~NOK 160

Fridtjof Nansen wanted a ship that would not be crushed by pack ice, and asked the Scottish-Norwegian designer Colin Archer to build one that would instead be squeezed upward out of it. The result, launched in 1892, has a rounded hull with almost no keel, oak sides more than a metre thick in places, and interior bracing everywhere. It is probably the strongest wooden vessel ever constructed.

Nansen then deliberately froze her into the ice north of Siberia in 1893 and let the current carry her for three years, hoping to drift across the pole. He did not reach it — he left the ship with one companion and a dog team, got to 86°14′N, and survived a winter in a stone hut on Franz Josef Land. Fram drifted out intact, as designed.

Roald Amundsen took the same ship south in 1910 and won the race to the South Pole. Fram has therefore been both further north and further south than any other wooden ship, and she is here, complete, with the museum constructed around her.

What to do inside

Go aboard and go below — the cabins, the galley, the saloon with its organ are all as fitted. The hull is close enough to touch and the scale of the timber is the thing you remember. There is a walk-through polar simulator that is genuinely cold, and the second hall holds Gjøa, the sloop in which Amundsen first sailed the Northwest Passage.
