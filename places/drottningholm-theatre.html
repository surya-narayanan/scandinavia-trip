Drottningholm Court Theatre

Drottningholms slottsteater

A 1766 theatre with its original wooden stage machinery still installed and still working — unique in the world.

Queen Lovisa Ulrika built the theatre in 1766, and her son Gustav III — genuinely stage-struck, a playwright and performer himself — made it the centre of Swedish theatrical life. Then in 1792 Gustav was shot at a masked ball at the Stockholm opera, died, and the court’s appetite for theatre died with him. Drottningholm was locked up and used for storage.

It stayed essentially untouched for 130 years. When it was rediscovered and reopened in 1921, the entire 18th-century stage apparatus was still in place: hand-cranked winches, wooden chariot-and-pole machinery for sliding painted flats in and out, rollers for wave effects, a thunder machine, trapdoors, and original painted scenery. Nowhere else in the world does this survive complete and functional.

What the tour shows you

The auditorium — which is not marble or gilt but painted wood and papier-mâché, faked convincingly because that is what the budget allowed — and then the machinery itself, demonstrated. A scene change that would take a modern crew minutes happens here in seconds, operated by rope and counterweight, with all the flats moving simultaneously.

It still hosts an opera season each summer, staged with period practice and lit to imitate candlelight. If a performance happens to fall on your date it is worth reorganising a day for.
