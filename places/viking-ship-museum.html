Viking Ship Museum

Five real Viking ships, deliberately sunk in the fjord a thousand years ago to block an invasion route.

Around 1070 the people of Roskilde sank five ships in the Peberrenden channel to block the approach to their town from the sea. They filled them with stones and scuttled them in a line. It worked, and it also preserved them: the ships sat in the mud until 1962, when a cofferdam was built around the site, the water pumped out, and the timbers excavated piece by piece.

What came up was not one type of vessel but five, which is why the find matters so much. There is a deep-sea trading knarr, a smaller coastal trader, a 30-metre longship built for war near Dublin from Irish oak, a smaller warship, and a fishing boat. Together they show a whole maritime economy rather than just the raiding that the Vikings are remembered for.

What you actually see

The originals are in the Viking Ship Hall, mounted on steel frames that hold the surviving timbers in the shape of the hull — perhaps a quarter of each ship survives, and the frames let you read the rest. It is an honest kind of display and more affecting than a full replica would be.

Outside on the harbour is the working half: a boatyard where shipwrights build full-size sailing reconstructions using period tools and techniques, and a fleet of them moored at the quay. The Sea Stallion from Glendalough, a reconstruction of the big Dublin-built longship, sailed to Ireland and back in 2007–08.
