Norwegian Museum of Cultural History

Norsk Folkemuseum

160 buildings moved here from all over Norway, including a stave church from about 1200.

The open-air museum was a late-19th-century Scandinavian invention: as rural building traditions collapsed, whole farmsteads were dismantled, moved and reassembled as a national collection. Norsk Folkemuseum, founded in 1894 on Bygdøy, is one of the largest, with about 160 structures from every region — turf-roofed mountain farms, storehouses on stilts, a whole Oslo tenement block with apartments furnished decade by decade from 1879 to 2002.

The building everyone comes for is the Gol stave church, built around 1200 in Hallingdal and moved here in 1885 at the initiative of King Oscar II. Stave churches are timber-framed on load-bearing corner posts, and of the roughly thousand that once stood in Norway, 28 remain. The dark tarred wood, dragon-head gables and carved portals are the real thing, not a reconstruction.

What to look for

The Setesdal farmstead buildings, some of the oldest surviving secular wooden buildings in Europe. The apartment block, which is more affecting than it sounds — you walk from an 1879 workers’ room into a 1950s kitchen into a 2002 flat. And in summer, staff in period dress doing actual work: baking flatbread, keeping livestock, demonstrating crafts.
