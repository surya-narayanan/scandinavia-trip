MUNCH

Thirteen floors on the Oslo waterfront holding the largest collection of one artist’s work anywhere.

Edvard Munch left almost everything still in his possession to the city of Oslo when he died in 1944 — roughly 1,150 paintings, 17,800 prints, 4,500 drawings, plus letters, photographs and his own printing plates. It is the largest bequest of one artist’s work to one city in European history, and it is the reason this museum exists at all.

The new building opened in 2021 next to the Opera House: thirteen floors, the upper section leaning noticeably toward the fjord, clad in perforated recycled aluminium. It was contentious in Oslo — the nickname "Lambda" and a good deal of local dislike of its bulk — and the interior is far better than the outside suggests.

The Scream, and the rest

There are three versions here — a painting, a pastel and a lithograph — and they are shown in rotation, one at a time, because the pigments cannot take continuous light. Do not plan around seeing a specific one.

The rest is the point, though. Munch worked on themes for decades, repainting the same subjects: The Sick Child, Madonna, The Dance of Life, the Frieze of Life cycle. There is a whole floor on his late work, which is far more colourful and stranger than his reputation suggests, and a room of the monumental University Aula studies. The top floors have a bar and a terrace with the best high view of the fjord in central Oslo.
