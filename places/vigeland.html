Vigeland Sculpture Park and Museum

Vigelandsanlegget og Vigelandmuseet

Two hundred and more sculptures of the human body by one artist, on one axis, all of it free and open at any hour.

Day 13 · Thu Aug 20 · 14:30–17:15Ninety minutes on flat gravel paths, reached by tram 12 from Øvre Slottsgate. It never closes and never charges, so it absorbs a late arrival without trouble.
Made1924–1943, by Gustav Vigeland
Works212 sculptures in bronze, granite and wrought iron
The Monolith17 m, 121 intertwined figures, carved from a single block
Park costFree, open 24 hours
MuseumNobels gate 32, across the road — Tue–Sun 10:00–17:00, closed Mondays

Gustav Vigeland made a deal with the city of Oslo in 1921: they would give him a studio and a salary, and everything he produced would become municipal property. He then spent two decades designing not just sculptures but the entire park around them — the layout, the bridges, the gates, the paving. It is one artist’s single unified work at the scale of a public park, which is close to unique.

The subject never changes: human beings, unclothed, at every age, in every relation to each other. Children, lovers, parents, old people, figures fighting and embracing and carrying one another. There is no mythology and no allegory to decode.

The three set pieces

The Bridge, lined with 58 bronzes, including the Angry Boy — a small furious child whose hand is worn gold by touching. The Fountain, a bronze basin held up by six giants, ringed by tree groups showing a life cycle. And at the top of the axis the Monolith: a 17-metre granite column of 121 figures climbing over one another, carved in place by three masons over 14 years, surrounded by 36 granite groups on the plateau.

Vigeland’s brother Emanuel painted the extraordinary mausoleum on the other side of the city; Gustav himself is buried in the tower of the museum at the park’s edge.

The Museum, which is a different place

People say “Vigeland” and mean the park, but there are two things here and they sit on opposite sides of the same road. The park is the finished work. The Vigeland Museum, at Nobels gate 32, is where it was made — it was Gustav Vigeland’s own studio and home, built for him by the city as its half of the 1921 bargain, and he lived and worked in it until he died in 1943.

Inside is the workings: the original plaster models for the figures you then walk out and meet full size, along with casts, woodcuts, drawings and his tools. His apartment on the top floor is kept as it was. The scale of the models is what lands — the Monolith’s 121 figures existed in plaster inside this building before a single one was cut in granite.

  • Open: Tuesday–Sunday 10:00–17:00. Closed Mondays.
  • Cost: NOK 80 adult, NOK 40 seniors, students and children 7–17, free under 7.
  • Time needed: about an hour.
  • Where: Nobels gate 32, directly across the road from the park.

Do the museum first and the park second. The clock forces it — the museum closes at 17:00 and the park never closes — but it is also the better order to experience, because you meet the models before the monuments rather than the other way round.
