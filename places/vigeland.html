Vigeland Sculpture Park

Vigelandsanlegget

Two hundred and more sculptures of the human body by one artist, on one axis, all of it free and open at any hour.

Day 13 · Thu Aug 20 · 15:15–16:45Ninety minutes on flat gravel paths, reached by tram 12 from Øvre Slottsgate. It never closes and never charges, so it absorbs a late arrival without trouble.
Made1924–1943, by Gustav Vigeland
Works212 sculptures in bronze, granite and wrought iron
The Monolith17 m, 121 intertwined figures, carved from a single block
CostFree, open 24 hours

Gustav Vigeland made a deal with the city of Oslo in 1921: they would give him a studio and a salary, and everything he produced would become municipal property. He then spent two decades designing not just sculptures but the entire park around them — the layout, the bridges, the gates, the paving. It is one artist’s single unified work at the scale of a public park, which is close to unique.

The subject never changes: human beings, unclothed, at every age, in every relation to each other. Children, lovers, parents, old people, figures fighting and embracing and carrying one another. There is no mythology and no allegory to decode.

The three set pieces

The Bridge, lined with 58 bronzes, including the Angry Boy — a small furious child whose hand is worn gold by touching. The Fountain, a bronze basin held up by six giants, ringed by tree groups showing a life cycle. And at the top of the axis the Monolith: a 17-metre granite column of 121 figures climbing over one another, carved in place by three masons over 14 years, surrounded by 36 granite groups on the plateau.

Vigeland’s brother Emanuel painted the extraordinary mausoleum on the other side of the city; Gustav himself is buried in the tower of the museum at the park’s edge.
