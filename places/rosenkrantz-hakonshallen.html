Rosenkrantz Tower and Håkon’s Hall

Rosenkrantztårnet og Håkonshallen

A royal hall from the 1260s and a tower where the king’s bedchamber sits directly above a dungeon.

Bergen was the capital of Norway in the 13th century, and this was the royal seat. Håkonshallen was built between 1247 and 1261 as the great hall of Håkon Håkonsson — the largest secular medieval building in Norway, put up for his son’s wedding and coronation feast. It is a plain, enormous stone room, and standing in it is the closest you will get to the Norwegian high middle ages.

The Rosenkrantz Tower next to it has a stranger history. Erik Rosenkrantz, the Danish governor, built it in the 1560s — but he built it by wrapping a Renaissance residence around two existing structures: a keep from around 1270 and a later fortified building. Three buildings from three centuries are fused into one, and you can read the joins in the masonry as you climb.

Why the tower is worth the stairs

Because of the vertical contrast, which is unusually blunt. Near the top are the governor’s and king’s chambers — fireplaces, window seats, privies built into the wall thickness, a room someone lived comfortably in. Directly below, in the base of the older keep, is the dungeon: no light, no ventilation, reached through a hatch. Same building, same stair, four centuries of Norwegian power in about sixty vertical metres.

1944

On 20 April 1944 the Dutch cargo ship Voorbode, loaded with 124 tonnes of explosives, blew up at the quay a short distance away. It killed around 160 people, flattened much of the district, and gutted Håkonshallen — the roof and interior were destroyed. Both buildings were restored over the following two decades, which is why the hall’s interior fittings are modern inside medieval walls.
