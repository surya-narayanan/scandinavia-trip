The Vasa Museum

Vasamuseet

A 64-gun warship that sank twenty minutes into her maiden voyage in 1628, raised in 1961, and 98 per cent original.

Gustavus Adolphus wanted the most powerfully armed ship in the Baltic and ordered two gun decks where one had been planned. On 10 August 1628 Vasa left the naval yard on her maiden voyage, caught a gust, heeled over, took water through her open gunports and sank in Stockholm harbour after sailing perhaps 1,300 metres. Around thirty people died. She was top-heavy and under-ballasted, and the inquiry that followed carefully avoided blaming the king who had specified her.

She then sat in the harbour mud for 333 years. The brackish, cold, oxygen-poor water of the Baltic has no shipworm, so the oak survived. Anders Franzén located her in 1956, and she was raised in 1961 in one piece — an extraordinary salvage operation — and sprayed with polyethylene glycol for seventeen years to keep the waterlogged timber from collapsing as it dried.

Why it is overwhelming in person

Because it is not a reconstruction. About 98 per cent of what you are looking at is the original 1628 ship, and the museum is built tightly around it across several levels, so you walk up alongside the hull and meet it at every height. She carried around 500 carved and originally painted sculptures — lions, emperors, mermaids, grotesques — most of which survive, and a model shows the colours they would have been.

The human material is the part that stays with you. Skeletons of some of the dead were recovered with the ship, along with their clothing, tools, coins and a sailmaker’s kit, and facial reconstructions of several are displayed with what could be worked out about their lives.
