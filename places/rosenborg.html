Christian IV built Rosenborg as a summer house just outside the city ramparts, starting in 1606 and extending it until 1633. It is small, red-brick, Dutch Renaissance in style, and it is the most personal of the Danish royal buildings — the king died in one of its rooms in 1648, and that room is preserved.

It stopped being a residence in the 18th century, which is precisely why it survives as it does: the interiors were left largely intact rather than modernised, and the building became a chronological museum of the monarchy, room by room, reign by reign.

The Treasury

The reason to come is in the basement. The Danish crown regalia are here — the crown of Christian IV, the absolutist crown of Christian V with its enormous sapphires and garnets, the sceptre, the orb, the sword of state, and the pearl and diamond sets still used by the royal family. Unlike most European regalia these remain in official use, which is why they are shown here rather than in a national museum.

Upstairs, and outside

The Long Hall on the top floor is the showpiece: the coronation thrones, three silver lions standing guard, and a ceiling of stucco and tapestry. Look for the marble chamber and Christian IV’s private writing closet on the way up.

Kongens Have, the King’s Garden, wraps around the castle and is the oldest royal garden in Denmark and the most used park in the city. It is free, flat and full of benches — the right place to sit for twenty minutes afterwards.
