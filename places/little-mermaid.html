The Little Mermaid

Den lille Havfrue

A 1.25-metre bronze on a rock at the harbour’s edge, and the most photographed and most vandalised object in Denmark.

Carl Jacobsen of the Carlsberg brewery saw a ballet of Andersen’s The Little Mermaid at the Royal Theatre in 1909 and commissioned a statue of its heroine. Edvard Eriksen modelled the head on the ballerina Ellen Price and, when she declined to pose nude, the body on his own wife Eline. The bronze was given to the city and unveiled on the Langelinie promenade in August 1913.

She is small — a metre and a quarter — and that is the single most common complaint about her. She sits on a granite boulder a few metres offshore, looking out over the water, in a pose that reads as wistful rather than triumphant. Andersen’s original story ends with the mermaid dissolving into sea foam, not marrying the prince, and the statue belongs to that version.

A long history of being attacked

She has been decapitated twice, in 1964 and 1998, lost her right arm to a saw in 1984, been blown off her rock with explosives in 2003, and been painted red, pink, blue and white on various occasions in support of various causes. The head stolen in 1964 was never recovered; the current one is a recast. The city has repeatedly floated moving her further offshore to make her harder to reach, and has repeatedly not done it.

What to look for

Come at either end of the day and she is a genuinely affecting small sculpture in an open harbour setting, with Kastellet’s ramparts behind you and the Øresund shipping lane in front. Come at eleven in the morning and she is a scrum. That is the whole visit, honestly assessed.
