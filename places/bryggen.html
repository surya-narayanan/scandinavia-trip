Bryggen

The Hanseatic wharf: a row of painted gables hiding a warren of leaning timber galleries behind.

Bergen was founded around 1070 and became the biggest port in Scandinavia, on one commodity: dried cod from northern Norway, traded south for grain. From 1360 the German merchants of the Hanseatic League ran that trade from this wharf as one of their four principal overseas Kontors, and they held it until 1754.

The men who worked here lived under a monastic discipline — no women allowed in the buildings, no cooking fires in the wooden warehouses, apprentices bound for years, everything organised by the Schøtstuene assembly halls up the hill where they ate and held their courts.

What is actually old

Bryggen has burned to the ground many times, most recently in 1702 and again in 1955, and each time it was rebuilt on the same plot lines using the same techniques. So the buildings you see are largely 18th-century and later — but the foundations and the layout run back to the 12th century, and that continuity, not the timber, is what UNESCO listed. Below ground the archaeological layers are metres deep; the 1955 fire site was excavated and is now Bryggens Museum.

Go behind the front

The painted facades are the photograph, but the interest is in the passages between the rows — narrow wooden lanes with galleries leaning inward overhead, open stairs, and the whole structure visibly out of true after three centuries of settling. There are workshops, small galleries and craftspeople in among them. Walk the length of one passage and back through another; it takes twenty minutes and it is the real thing.
