Bryggen

The Hanseatic wharf: a row of painted gables hiding a warren of leaning timber galleries behind.

Bergen was founded around 1070 and became the biggest port in Scandinavia, on one commodity: dried cod from northern Norway, traded south for grain. From 1360 the German merchants of the Hanseatic League ran that trade from this wharf as one of their four principal overseas Kontors, and they held it until 1754.

The men who worked here lived under a monastic discipline — no women allowed in the buildings, no cooking fires in the wooden warehouses, apprentices bound for years, everything organised by the Schøtstuene assembly halls up the hill where they ate and held their courts.

What is actually old

Bryggen has burned to the ground many times, most recently in 1702 and again in 1955, and each time it was rebuilt on the same plot lines using the same techniques. So the buildings you see are largely 18th-century and later — but the foundations and the layout run back to the 12th century, and that continuity, not the timber, is what UNESCO listed. Below ground the archaeological layers are metres deep; the 1955 fire site was excavated and is now Bryggens Museum.

Go behind the front

The painted facades are the photograph, but the interest is in the passages between the rows — narrow wooden lanes with galleries leaning inward overhead, open stairs, and the whole structure visibly out of true after three centuries of settling. There are workshops, small galleries and craftspeople in among them. Walk the length of one passage and back through another; it takes twenty minutes and it is the real thing.

What to walk into, specifically

Bryggen is not one building. It is a row of separate tenements, each with its own name and its own passage running back from the quay, and the names are on plaques at the passage mouths — which is how locals navigate it. These are the ones worth stepping into:

  • Jacobsfjorden — look for the gilded deer head mounted on the front facade, which is why it is also called Hjortegården, the Deer Courtyard. The single most findable detail on the whole wharf, and the easiest way to orient yourself.
  • Bellgården — around three hundred years old, and it forms a double courtyard with Jacobsfjorden. Go in through one passage and out through the other; that one manoeuvre is the best two minutes at Bryggen.
  • Bredsgården — one of the best-preserved passages, and the 18th-century dining rooms of Bryggen Tracteursted open off it. Worth stepping inside even if you are not eating.
  • Enhjørningsgården — the Unicorn Courtyard.
  • Svensgården, Bugården, Holmedalsgården and Engelgården — the rest of the surviving rows, and the ones with the most visible lean.
  • Finnegården, Bryggen 39 — the Hanseatic Museum building. Under restoration until about 2027, so note the facade and do not queue at the door.
  • Øvregaten — the back lane running behind the entire wharf, and the street the Fjellveien descent comes down onto. Schøtstuene is at number 50 and Mariakirken is at its northern end.

A walking route you can open on your phone

Open the Bryggen walk in Google Maps → It runs Fish Market → Bryggen → Bryggens Museum → Mariakirken → Schøtstuene → Rosenkrantz Tower, on foot, which is the spine of the whole area and the exact order the Aug 18 plan uses.

One caveat worth knowing: Maps will not route you into the passages themselves. They are short, partly private, dead-end lanes, so they do not appear as walkable ways — the route line runs along the quay and Øvregaten instead. Treat the passages as twenty-metre detours off that line, taken on foot by reading the name plaques as you pass them.
