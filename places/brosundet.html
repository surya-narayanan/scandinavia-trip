Brosundet and Art Nouveau Ålesund

A town that burned to the ground in one night in 1904 and was rebuilt, all at once, in Art Nouveau.

On the night of 23 January 1904 a fire started in a herring oil factory, the wind took it, and by morning almost the entire town centre of Ålesund had burned — 850 houses gone, 10,000 people homeless in a Norwegian January. Remarkably, one person died.

Kaiser Wilhelm II, who holidayed in the Sunnmøre fjords and had a genuine affection for the place, sent four ships with materials and shelter within days. Reconstruction was immediate and coordinated, and because it happened in a single burst between 1904 and 1907, it happened in one style: Jugendstil, the German and Nordic strand of Art Nouveau, executed by young Norwegian architects mostly trained abroad.

The result is a small city of about 350 Art Nouveau buildings — turrets, curved gables, stylised plant and animal ornament, dragon motifs borrowed from stave churches — which is one of the most complete Art Nouveau townscapes anywhere in Europe.

What to look for

Walk the Brosundet canal, which cuts through the middle of town and is lined with former warehouses now converted to hotels and restaurants. Look up: the ornament is almost all above ground-floor level, on gables, window surrounds and doorways. The Swan Pharmacy building on Apotekergata, now the Art Nouveau Centre, is the single best-preserved example.
