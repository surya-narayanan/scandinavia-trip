Gefion Fountain and St Alban’s Church

Gefionspringvandet

A Norse creation myth in bronze, with an English neo-gothic spire standing directly behind it.

The fountain illustrates the founding myth of Zealand. The goddess Gefion was promised as much land as she could plough in a night; she turned her four sons into oxen, cut the island of Zealand out of Sweden, and dragged it into the sea — leaving, the story goes, the lake Vänern behind as the hole. Anders Bundgaard’s bronze catches the oxen mid-strain, steam coming off them, which is why it works as a photograph.

It was a gift from the Carlsberg Foundation, unveiled in 1908, and it is the largest monument in Copenhagen. The water was intended to run in stages down the terraces below the group.

St Alban’s

Directly behind the fountain stands St Alban’s, the English church in Copenhagen, built 1885–87 in Kentish ragstone in a strict English Gothic Revival style for the city’s British community — and for Princess Alexandra of Denmark, who had married the future Edward VII. Its spire lines up almost exactly behind the oxen from the standard viewpoint, and the two together are the shot people come here for.

Kastellet, the star-shaped 17th-century citadel, is immediately behind: grass ramparts, a moat, a working windmill, and flat paths. It is free, open and almost always calmer than the waterfront a few hundred metres away.
