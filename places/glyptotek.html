Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek

A brewer’s private collection of antiquities and French sculpture, arranged around a domed palm garden.

Day 5 · Wed Aug 12 · 10:00–11:10Five minutes from your door and it opens at 10:00, which makes it the right first stop on a departure day. Just over an hour is enough for the Winter Garden, the Rodins and the Roman portrait heads without rushing.
Founded1888, by Carl Jacobsen of Carlsberg
StrengthsEgyptian, Greek and Roman sculpture; Rodin; French and Danish 19th-century painting
The centrepieceThe Winter Garden, under a glass dome
Cost~DKK 135, free with the Copenhagen Card
Opens10:00. Five minutes from your apartment

Carl Jacobsen inherited a brewery and spent its profits on sculpture. He gave his collection to the public in 1888 on the condition that the state and city house it, and the resulting museum — glyptothek, a collection of carved stone — opened in 1897 and was extended in 1906. It remains one of the most personal major museums in Europe, because the shape of it is still one man’s taste.

That taste ran two ways. On one side, classical antiquity: Egyptian, Greek, Etruscan and Roman work, including one of the world’s better collections of Roman portrait busts and the Palmyra reliefs. On the other, contemporary French: he bought Rodin early, and the museum has one of the largest Rodin holdings outside Paris, alongside Degas bronzes and a strong group of French and Danish 19th-century painting.

The Winter Garden

The middle of the building is a glass-domed courtyard planted with palms, with a fountain and a café among the sculpture. It is the single most photographed interior in Copenhagen and it is also just a very good place to sit. Jacobsen’s reasoning was frankly commercial: he believed people who would not come for antiquities would come for the garden, and then look at the antiquities.

What to look for

Rodin’s Burghers of Calais and a cast of The Kiss; the Egyptian collection in the basement, including a hippopotamus statuette that has become the museum’s mascot; and the Roman heads, which are worth walking slowly past because they are portraits of real, specific, often visibly unpleasant people.
