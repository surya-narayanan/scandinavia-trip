Amalienborg

Four identical rococo mansions around an octagonal courtyard — the home of the Danish monarch, and an accident of history.

Frederik V laid out a new district in the 1750s on land that had been the Amalienborg garden, and the plan was rigorous: two axes crossing at an octagon, with four matching palaces at the corners built by four noble families at their own expense, to the design of the court architect Nicolai Eigtved. The royal family had no intention of living here.

Then in 1794 Christiansborg Palace burned down. The court bought the four mansions as an emergency measure and never left. That is why the Danish monarch lives in what is architecturally a set of private houses rather than a palace — and why the four wings still carry their original owners’ names.

The changing of the guard

The Royal Life Guards march from Rosenborg Castle through the city and reach the courtyard at noon. If the monarch is in residence the guard is accompanied by the regimental band, and the royal standard flies from the palace — check the flagpole to know which version you are getting. The ceremony is free, unticketed and takes about half an hour, and the crowd forms from roughly 11:40.

What to look for

Stand in the middle of the octagon and look along both axes: one runs to the Marble Church, the other to the harbour and across to the Opera House. The equestrian statue of Frederik V at the centre took the sculptor Jacques Saly nearly twenty years and cost more than any one of the four palaces.
