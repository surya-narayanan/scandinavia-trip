Nyhavn

A 17th-century naval canal lined with the painted townhouses that became the image of Copenhagen.

Day 2 · Sun Aug 9 · 08:45–09:45, and again 16:40–17:10You get it twice in one day, deliberately: an empty quay at 08:45 before the crowds, then again at 16:40 on the way home from Rosenborg with the light reversed. Same houses, opposite sun, and the early frames are already safe by the time it fills up.
Dug1670–1673, by Swedish prisoners of war
What it wasA working commercial harbour, and for 300 years the city’s roughest quarter
CostFree — it is a street
Best lightEast end looking west in the morning; west end looking east in late afternoon

Christian V had Nyhavn cut in the early 1670s to bring cargo ships from the Øresund directly to Kongens Nytorv, the new square at the centre of his city. The labour was Swedish prisoners taken in the war of 1658–60. It worked as intended for two centuries: this was a commercial harbour, and the gabled houses along it were warehouses, chandlers, sailors’ lodgings, tattoo parlours and taverns.

That is worth knowing while you photograph it, because the postcard prettiness is recent. Into the 1960s Nyhavn was a byword for cheap drink and fights, avoided by respectable Copenhageners. The north side was pedestrianised in 1980, the wooden ships were brought in as a museum fleet, and the quay became what you see now.

What to look for

The colours are the point, and they are on the north side — which means they face south, and you shoot them from the south quay. The oldest house is No. 9, from 1681, with the steep stepped gable. Hans Christian Andersen lived at three different addresses on this canal: No. 20, where he wrote his first fairy tales in 1835, No. 67, and No. 18, where he spent his last years.

The wooden ships moored along the canal are a working heritage fleet rather than props — lightships, schooners and a former Greenland trader. At the harbour end the canal opens onto Inderhavnen and the modern footbridge across to Christianshavn.

The practical bit

It never closes and costs nothing, which is why timing is the only real decision. Crowds build from about 10:00 and the quay-front restaurants charge accordingly — the side streets one block back are better value and quieter.
