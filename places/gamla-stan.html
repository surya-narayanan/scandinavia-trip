The medieval island the city grew from — and the cathedral where Swedish monarchs were crowned.

Stockholm began here in 1252, on the island of Stadsholmen where Lake Mälaren empties into the Baltic — a controllable choke point on the iron trade. The street plan is still essentially medieval: narrow lanes running off a spine, packed tight, with ochre and rust-coloured buildings mostly rebuilt after fires but on their original plots.

Stortorget and the Bloodbath

The main square is the oldest in the city and looks entirely charming, which makes its history jarring. In November 1520 Christian II of Denmark had around eighty Swedish nobles, clergy and burghers beheaded here over three days after promising them amnesty — the Stockholm Bloodbath. It triggered the rebellion that broke Danish rule and put Gustav Vasa on the throne, so it is arguably the founding event of independent Sweden. The white stones set into the facade of one house are said, probably wrongly, to count the dead.

Storkyrkan

The cathedral, immediately beside the palace, dates from the 13th century and is where Swedish monarchs were crowned. The thing to see is Saint George and the Dragon, a huge carved and painted oak group of 1489 by Bernt Notke — commissioned to commemorate a victory over the Danes, and unusually violent and detailed for a devotional object, with real elk antlers on the dragon.

Also

Mårten Trotzigs gränd, the narrowest alley in the city at 90 cm, is a set of steps between two buildings. Riddarholmen, the small island next door, holds the royal burial church and is much quieter than Stadsholmen.
